# Revision protocol

Use this whenever something material changes: new evidence, contrary evidence already in the record is raised, an assumption is removed, an objective changes, a deadline passes, or a legal proposition turns out wrong.

## 1. Classify the change

- **Record fact**: a new or newly noticed document. Cite it.
- **Contrary evidence already in the record**: cite it, and say why it was underweighted before.
- **Assumption removed**: name it by ID (A1, A2...).
- **Objective changed**: is this the client's actual change (source?) or a **HYPOTHETICAL** for testing?
- **Legal change**: a proposition was verified false, or newly verified.

Label hypotheticals in every document they touch, and keep them out of the factual columns. Ask whether the student wants the hypothetical analysis kept as a separate branch or used to replace the current version.

## 2. Trace the effect

Walk through the working documents in order and mark each item **unaffected**, **weakened**, **strengthened** or **invalidated**:

1. Chronology and actors: does the change alter what happened, who did it, or when? Recompute any clock it touches.
2. Propositions and claims: which elements move?
3. Competing theories: which theory does the change favor? Does the distinguishing evidence change?
4. Options: for each path, re-run the stress test (contrary evidence, opposing response, failed assumption, cross-forum effects).
5. Recommendation and sequence: does the preferred path still beat the strongest alternative? Does the first step change?

Show this as a short table: item, before, after, reason (with locator).

## 3. Decide, and say so plainly

One of:
- **Holds**: and why the change doesn't move it.
- **Holds with modification**: what changes in the sequence, risks or reassessment points.
- **Changes**: the new preferred path, and why it now beats the old one.
- **Uncertain**: what single step would resolve the uncertainty, and what to do meanwhile.

If the recommendation does not change after a change that was supposed to be material, check yourself: either explain convincingly why it is robust, or admit that the earlier recommendation was not actually resting on the premise you thought it was.

## 4. Engage the student

Before showing your full trace, ask the student: *Which parts of our analysis do you expect this to affect, and why?* Compare their answer with your trace. Discuss the differences.

## 5. Log it

Add a change-log row (version, date, trigger, what changed, why, documents affected), increment the version, and output the updated documents for saving. Never silently overwrite an earlier conclusion; the earlier reasoning and the reason for leaving it are part of the work.
