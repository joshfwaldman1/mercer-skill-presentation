**Question 3.** Compare your claim with the rule quoted above. Does the panel have to find pretext for Mercer to win? Does proving pretext win the hearing on its own?

### SIMULATED STUDENT

> Pretext is how you prove "arbitrary and capricious." If the reasons are fake, the decision is arbitrary by definition. So yes, proving pretext wins. And we have to go after leadership's motive, otherwise the panel just defers to the doctors' review.

## Claude

Half right. The rule never mentions motive, pretext or bad faith. It asks whether the action "lacks substantial basis" or whether its conclusions are "arbitrary, unreasonable, or capricious" (Bylaws VIII §5.i p.31). That mismatch costs you in two ways.

1. **You take on a burden the rule doesn't impose.** To show pretext, you must prove what Calder, Baxter, Doran and Whitcomb were thinking. The file can't do that today (Q1, element (c) is OPEN), and discovery is limited to the evidence the hospital "intends to present" (Bylaws VIII §3.d; 04 §A). Counsel cannot examine witnesses, so Mercer would have to cross-examine the people he is accusing (Bylaws VIII §5.e; 04 §A).
2. **Pretext doesn't win by itself.** The panel can believe leadership wanted him gone *and* still find a substantial basis in 27 of 27 packets plus the admitted H&P (R2/Summary pp.3-4; R2/Notes). A real motive and a real basis can coexist.

**What the rule does reward is already in your evidence.** The notebook's statement of the arbitrariness ground lists "a process that was predetermined" as a way to show it (04 H2). A decision made before its evidence existed is predetermined, whatever the motive was. The "lacks substantial basis" ground is where ICRO lives (04 H1).

Pretext is an element elsewhere. It belongs to the §2801-b claim (reasons "unrelated to standards of patient care", 04 O1), to rebutting HCQIA immunity (04 O11), to age discrimination (04 O9) and to retaliation (04 O10). Whether to save the motive accusation for those forums is a strategy choice for you and the client. It also bears on tone before a panel the Board appoints (Bylaws VIII §4.b p.30).

## The verdict: **Revise**

The claim fails question 3 and only partly survives question 4. The rule doesn't ask about motive, the file holds no proof of a motive beyond inference, and the hospital's admitted-H&P account predicts the same timeline. The evidence survives intact. Only the label changes. The timing that can't prove a hidden motive does prove a decision made before its evidence, which is what "arbitrary" means under VIII §5.i.

**Revised claim:** The Board's January 21 termination rests on conclusions that are arbitrary and lack substantial basis under Bylaws Art. VIII §5.i, because leadership issued a without-cause notice on December 12 and suspended Dr. Mercer as an imminent danger on December 16 before any chart review existed, and the only independent review of the charts it later sent out found the standard of care met.

- **What it gives up.** The claim that leadership's real reason was something else, whether call pay, the physician dispute or retaliation. Mercer no longer has to prove what Calder, Baxter, Doran or Whitcomb were thinking, so he doesn't have to cross-examine them on it. If motive evidence turns up, pretext can still be run in the forums where it is an element: §2801-b, HCQIA rebuttal, ADEA, and Labor Law §740 (04 O1, O9, O10, O11). Whether to do that is your call and the client's.
- **What it keeps.** Every timing fact, the ICRO ratings, the MEC's sustain-only recommendation, the 14-minute session, Packet 26 after termination, and the 14-day lapse.
