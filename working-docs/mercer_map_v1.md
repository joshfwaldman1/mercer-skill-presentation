## Competing theories
| Theory | One sentence | 3 facts it needs | Most dangerous fact | Answer | Distinguishing evidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Mercer: pretextual, defective process** | Leadership decided to end him and built the case after the fact; the only independent reviewer found the standard met | ICRO ratings; the 12/11 → 12/12 → 12/16 sequence; Board exceeding the MEC | His own H&P admissions | Admitted at once; already disciplined by correction; not in the 1/21 grounds | See below |
| **Hospital: substantial basis** | A chair's review of 77+ charts found concerns throughout, on top of an admitted falsified H&P and a conduct complaint | 27 of 27 packets; the admissions; the deferential standard and the burden on Mercer | ICRO, its own outside reviewer | ICRO looked at only 2 "expedited" charts; Case B had "some concerns" | |
| **Third: real but overreacted** | Real documentation and screening problems warranted correction, which the hospital chose on 12/11; the escalation to imminent danger and termination outran the evidence | Plan of Correction; MEC sustain-only; ICRO | Volume of packet concerns | Concerns ≠ deviations (only 3 labeled) | |

**Distinguishing evidence.** The best single item is what actually happened on 10/31-11/3: whether Mercer examined the patient, whether the bill was submitted and refunded, and what the badge and video logs show (G10, G19).
- If Mercer never examined her and the bill wasn't refunded, the hospital theory's anchor fact is real fraud exposure. That favors negotiation and minimizing his testimony.
- If the H&P was a records-prompted late entry for a patient he managed by phone and the bill was refunded, the third theory dominates. That makes the hearing and leverage paths stronger.
- Second-best: what the Board saw on 1/21 (G5). If ICRO was withheld, P2 strengthens sharply.
